# Experiment update — 13/10/2026

I have finished the seed 6 runs for EWC, MAS and RWalk. For each method, I tested reckless and cautious BrainWash at budgets of 0.1 and 0.3, ran the feature ablations, and evaluated Rank and MMD. The other runs were still in progress when I exported these results.

The threshold worked reasonably well with EWC: none of the clean simulations triggered an alert, and most simulations containing 8 poisoned images out of 150 were detected. MAS and RWalk had threshold-transfer failures. MAS missed most poisoned images, even when ROC-AUC was high. RWalk ranked the samples well at the larger attack budget, but flagged every clean simulation.

In the ablations, the tensor gradient norms accounted for most of the useful discrimination. Rank reduced the clean alerts compared with MMD, but detected little at small poisoning fractions. In some settings, increasing the poisoning fraction actually reduced its detection rate.

I still need seeds 7 and 8 to see whether these patterns repeat.

## 2. Experimental Setup

### 2.1 Completed Runs and Task Roles

| Component | Setting |

Tasks are numbered from zero. I extract the features before the defender learns the incoming task, so the model used for Task 9 has learned Tasks 0–8.

I trained a separate detector for each CL method on 3,000 Task 1 images. For each image, I used the clean version as a negative and the reckless BrainWash version at budget 0.3 as a positive. The training set therefore has 6,000 rows, drawn from 3,000 originals. I kept random perturbations out of detector training.

I split each task into 3,000 training, 500 validation, 1,000 reserve and 500 test originals. The clean, poisoned and random versions of an image all stay in the same split. For sample evaluation, I used the clean and poisoned versions of the 500 test images. I did not use the other Task 9 splits to fit or calibrate the detector.

After the seed 5 pilot reported on 04/10, I fixed `history_mad` for this experiment before starting the runs. I used the Task 4 test split to check the rule, without selecting a new one from those results. I also saved the source-only and historical-quantile results for comparison.

### 2.3 Features

The full supervised detector uses 84 inputs:

| Feature | Dimensions | Meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Parameter-tensor gradient norms | 80 | L2 norm of the loss gradient for each backbone weight or bias tensor |
| Entropy | 1 | How spread out the head's predicted class probabilities are |
| Confidence | 1 | Largest predicted probability |
| Margin | 1 | Difference between the two largest predicted probabilities |
| Activation norm | 1 | L2 norm of the representation entering the classification head |

I compute the gradients using the model's predicted class as the loss target. The classifier receives no true-class probability, loss or historical-gradient cosine features. Image-class labels are still used to construct the benchmark and stratified splits; feature extraction and detector scoring do not read them.

The incoming head is initialised with a fixed seed and is untrained on the incoming task. I use its prediction to obtain the gradients, so its entropy and confidence describe this untrained head. I keep the model in evaluation mode during extraction, without changing the backbone parameters or BatchNorm state.

## 3. Frozen Thresholds

I first score the clean validation images from Tasks 1 and 4. For each task, I convert the detector scores to logits and calculate the median and scaled median absolute deviation. I use a multiplier of three for the robust cutoff, compare it with the clean quantile cutoff, and keep the larger value. The final sample cutoff is the larger of the two task cutoffs.

Once the sample cutoff is fixed, I count alerts in the separate reserve-clean images. I calculate a one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper bound on the image alert rate for each task, then use the larger bound to set the dataset threshold. This is the smallest count whose binomial upper tail is at most 0.025. The error budget for estimating the image alert rate is also 0.025. An incoming simulation alerts when its flagged-image count reaches this threshold.

I apply this procedure separately to each fitted detector, giving the following cutoffs:

| CL method | Sample cutoff | Count cutoff out of 150 | Task 4 clean sample FPR | Task 4 clean dataset FPR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EWC | 0.985904 | 5 | 0.4% | 0.0% |
| MAS | 0.075256 | 7 | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| RWalk | 0.215121 | 6 | 0.2% | 0.0% |

The table rounds the sample cutoffs; evaluation uses the full stored values. All three detectors passed the clean-error check on Task 4. As the Task 9 results show, this check was not enough to ensure that the thresholds would transfer. The count calculation assumes that the clean image alert probability remains applicable on the incoming task.

## 7. Unsupervised Results

### 7.1 Rank and MMD Setup

For this part, I used neither labelled clean/poison training examples nor known-clean Task 9 calibration images.

I extracted ten descriptors for both tests: the global backbone gradient norm, five stage gradient norms, entropy, confidence, margin and activation norm. Each method's reference has 128 inversion samples from each of Tasks 0–8, or 1,152 samples in total. I extracted the reference and incoming features with the same Task 9 defender and fixed head.

Rank makes one decision for the whole simulation. I calculate the Kendall correlations between feature pairs and compare them with the correlations from each historical task. To choose the pairs, I use only the historical references. For each pair, I leave out one reference image at a time and calculate the jackknife pseudovalues. I keep the pair if these have nonzero estimated variation in every reference.

For each comparison, I take the largest absolute correlation difference across the retained pairs. I estimate its variation using 499 Gaussian multiplier bootstrap draws from the centred pseudovalues. The p-value is `(1 + number of bootstrap statistics at least as large as the observed statistic) / 500`. The simulation alerts only if every historical-task comparison rejects at 0.05. These draws are used to estimate statistical variation; the image dataset stays the same.

I kept the earlier MMD test as a baseline. It uses a random-Fourier approximation to a kernel two-sample test against the historical inversion references. MMD can respond to changes in feature magnitudes as well as other distribution differences. Rank compares pairwise dependence. Neither test can by itself establish that a difference between real incoming images and inversion samples is caused by poisoning.

MAS cautious budget 0.3 is a difficult case: detection is only 2% with 38 poisoned images and 18% with 75. It reaches 99% when all 150 images are poisoned.

The fully poisoned simulations may also resemble one of the historical references closely enough to avoid rejection. MMD flags every simulation: clean, poisoned and random-control, for all three methods. It provides no useful distinction between them in these runs.

They measure evidence against the reference comparison, not the probability of poisoning, and do not guarantee a 5% clean false-positive rate with inversion samples.

## 8. Attack Effectiveness and Random Perturbations

To check the attack effects, I continued the victim's training on the full incoming task in three conditions: clean, BrainWash-poisoned and uniformly perturbed images. I then measured forgetting. The smaller simulations in Sections 5 and 7 are only detector evaluations; they do not retrain the victim.

All four BrainWash settings reduce past accuracy for each method. MAS reckless budget 0.3 causes a drop of 23.93 percentage points, even though the detector misses most poisoned images. The attack is effective in this run; the detection failure cannot be attributed to a harmless perturbation.

The uniform perturbations also cause forgetting. At budget 0.3, RWalk loses 13.77 percentage points of past accuracy. This supports your point about random perturbations: they can still harm past tasks. I report their alert rates separately from the clean false-positive rates.

The full detector flags 0.6% of random-control images for EWC and 0% for MAS at both budgets. RWalk flags 21.8% at budget 0.1 and 83.0% at budget 0.3. All of its random-control simulations alert, but so do all of its clean simulations.

For the uniform training condition, I drew new perturbations at the same budget. They are not the exact random perturbations used in detector evaluation. I have measured the resulting forgetting here, but have not tested whether filtering with the detector improves continual learning.
